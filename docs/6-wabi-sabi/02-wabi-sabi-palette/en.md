# Wabi-sabi — the palette

---

Seven colours. One is the wall everything else sits on, one is the text, and five are the actual
palette. That is smaller than most families here, and small on purpose: the material argument only
holds if the palette is thin enough to be believed.

---

- **Lime plaster `#E8E0D5`** — the surface. A wall, a page ground, an uncoated sheet. The lightest
  colour and the only one meant to cover a large area, warm but only just: at `L` 0.91 it sits
  above the band, so it is not a main colour.
- **Sand `#C9BBA6`** — the first base. Dried, sieved, warm-neutral, the workhorse colour.
- **Clay `#A08C7D`** — the second base. Darker, redder, the first colour that clearly reads as
  earth.
- **Bark `#8C6D5B`** — the accent, and the one bold move. The most chromatic colour here at
  `C` 0.05, and the only one truly warm-orange.
- **Moss `#7D8471`** — the second accent, and the only cool-leaning hue. It keeps the palette from
  reading as one brown ramp.
- **Forest `#4A5A45`** — the third accent, and the dark end of the material story. Deep and green,
  for when the design must recede.
- **Ink `#3B342E`** — the text. A brown-black at `C` 0.01, never a fill, never an emphasis border.

**The tints are what actually get used for large surfaces.** The main colours are too dark or too
earthy to cover area — clay `#A08C7D` across a page reads as a heavy block, not a surface. The
tints are the practical palette: sand for a card background, clay for a divider, bark for muted
text.

Two properties make this work. First, chroma is already tiny at the base, so tints stay on the same
side of neutral as their parent: a sand tint is still warm, never a generic grey. Second, the dark
end goes further than you expect — bark at `L` 0.56 reaches into the deep browns, and forest at
`L` 0.45 goes darker still, giving room for a dark surface. But the ramp's shadows belong to the
ink's territory, so use them sparingly.

So: **build the layout out of tints, and reserve the base colours for small pieces**.

---

## 3. How the palette is put together

The bases are a ladder in warmth and depth: sand is lightest and least saturated, clay sits in the
middle and turns redder, bark is darkest and most chromatic. The accents go sideways rather than
down: bark stays brown, moss steps out to yellow-green, forest goes deep and cool.
Two independent axes: you can get *darker* without changing hue, and *cooler* without changing
lightness, but there is no third. There is no "cool and light" corner — lime plaster is closest, and
it is warm.

---

## 4. Extending it

The ceiling is so low that extending this palette is almost entirely a question of **lightness and
hue temperature**. Saturation is not available — there is nowhere to go.

### 4.1 Deriving from lightness

Take a base colour, hold its hue and chroma, and move `L`. Clay at `L` 0.65 gives a warm mid tone;
up to 0.80, a pale still-warm surface; down to 0.45, a deep earth. Every one is legitimately
wabi-sabi, because none added colour. This is the safest way to extend the family, and the ramp
exists for it.

### 4.2 Deriving from hue temperature

The second move is to slide the hue while holding lightness and chroma. A colour at `H` 60 and
`C` 0.03 can become one at `H` 90 — same lightness, same colour, but reading as olive, not brown.
The family has only four hues and you do not want a fifth: stay within about 20 degrees of an
existing hue and keep `C` at or below 0.05. Past that the new colour starts to name something
specific, and a name is how a family drifts.

### 4.3 What is not available

- **A brighter accent.** If the design needs a colour that pulls the eye hard, this family cannot
  supply it — there is no saturated anything here, by definition.
- **A cool light neutral.** A pale grey-blue second surface means leaving the family or faking it,
  and the fake shows.

### 4.4 The rule for new colours

If you must add a colour, add it in this order: lightness first, hue second, chroma last and only
ever downward. If a new colour needs chroma above 0.05, it is not a wabi-sabi colour, and the
problem it solves is better handled by the ink, the surface, or the type.

## 5. Pairing rules

**Surface with ink, always.** Ink `#3B342E` sits at a contrast ratio of 12.23 against white — well
past AA — and at 9.35 against lime plaster `#E8E0D5`, the surface it belongs on. Both clear the 4.5
threshold, so long text never needs pure black.

**Light body text goes on light fills.** Sand and clay, at `L` 0.80 and 0.65 both read as surfaces
and clear the 4.5 threshold with ink on top — what to build cards from.

**Bark is a borderline text colour.** At 4.45 against white it sits just *under* the 4.5 threshold,
and at 4.45 against black just over it — the same number both ways, because bark is a mid-lightness
colour. Not safe for body text on a light ground; barely passes on a dark one. Use it for large
text, borders and chart bars, where the threshold is 3.

**Forest is the safe dark.** At 7.40 against white it clears AA, and at 2.84 against black it is a
genuinely dark surface. On lime plaster it works for headings and muted panels.

**Moss is for surfaces and shapes, not text.** At 3.88 against white it passes the 3:1 threshold
for non-text elements and fails the 4.5 for body text. Use it for fills, chart series, numerals.

**Two accents at once is fine; three is not.** Bark and moss together is a conversation. Add forest
and you have a chart, not a page.

**Never two warm earths side by side at equal weight.** Sand and clay with no lightness gap merge;
keep one `L` step.

## 6. Common mistakes

### 6.1 Adding a saturated accent

The most common failure, and the most damaging. A designer wants "one colour that pops" and reaches
for a terracotta or a sage. That colour is outside the family's chroma ceiling, and once it is there
the palette reads as "muted" with one exception rather than as wabi-sabi. The palette already has an
accent — bark at `C` 0.05 is as loud as it gets.

### 6.2 Using the ink for fills

Ink `#3B342E` is text. Put it behind white text and you get a conventional dark panel that reads as
a different product. If you need a dark surface, use forest, a colour of this world, or a deep shade
of bark from the ramp. Ink behind ink-colored text is invisible; behind a light fill it leaves the
family.

### 6.3 Treating wabi-sabi as "the beige palette"

This produces the most beige. Beige is a place on the colour wheel; wabi-sabi is a
set of rules — four named hues, a named surface, a named ink, a chroma ceiling. Drop any and what
is left is a warm neutral palette, not this family. The tell: if you could describe the palette as
"brown, beige and grey", you have lost the green. Moss and forest are not decoration; they are why
the family reads as earth, not sepia.

### 6.4 A fourth mistake worth naming

Using lime plaster for text on white. It is a surface colour, and at 1.31 against white it is
barely visible. Captions and metadata should be clay, a bark shade, or forest — not the wall.
