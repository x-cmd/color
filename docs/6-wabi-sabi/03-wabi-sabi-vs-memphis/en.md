# Wabi-sabi versus memphis

---

These two get brought up together, and they are almost exact opposites: both reaction styles, both
anti-polish, both from the same wave of post-modern design writing. Then they point in opposite
directions on nearly every measurable axis. One lives in a ceramics studio; the other was founded in
Milan in 1981.

---

## 1. The short version

Wabi-sabi is **quiet, dark, warm and material**. Memphis is **loud, bright, synthetic and
graphic**. Wabi-sabi takes its colour from objects that exist: clay, bark, plaster, wood. Memphis
takes its colour from printed objects: laminates, terrazzo, plastic, screen ink.

The clearest number is chroma. Wabi-sabi's main colours run `C` 0.03 to 0.05 — a spread of 0.02.
Memphis runs `C` 0.12 to 0.25 — a spread of 0.13, roughly six times wider. Mean chroma: 0.04
against 0.19. Everything else follows from it.

**The HSL saturation row.** Wabi-sabi's mean HSL saturation is 16%, Memphis's is 91%. They are not

**The ground row.** Memphis is built on black and white: `#FFFFFF` and `#1A1A1A` are two of its
seven colours, the ground for the primaries. Wabi-sabi has neither a true black nor a true
white; its ground is lime plaster `#E8E0D5`, and its darkest colour, ink `#3B342E`, is a warm
brown-black at `C` 0.01.

## 3. Where they really differ

### 3.1 Polarity, not degree

The easy mistake is to treat this as a slider. They sit at opposite ends, and the axis is not
intensity but **polarity** — quiet vs loud, material vs graphic, aged vs new, one voice vs many.
"70% wabi-sabi, 30% memphis" gets nothing in between: a wabi-sabi palette with shouting colours
in it, which reads as a mistake both ways — too colourful for one, too washed-out for the other.

### 3.2 Where each one comes from

Memphis is a specific, datable thing. The Memphis Group was founded in Milan in 1981, around
Ettore Sottsass and colleagues, and its name comes from a Bob Dylan song. Its vocabulary is
reproducible: squiggles, terrazzo patterns, dot grids, laminate surfaces, saturated primaries
clashing on purpose.

Wabi-sabi is not a group and has no founding date. It is a Japanese aesthetic built around two
characters — 侘, plainness and deliberate austerity; 寂, patina and the visible record of time —
reaching back to the tea ceremony and its crafts. Both reject the tasteable, neutral
modernism of their era: Memphis by making noise, wabi-sabi by removing more.

### 3.3 Material versus print

This is the deepest difference and the one most often missed. A memphis colour is a *printed*
colour: it lands on a surface — a laminate, a poster, a screen — meant to stay flat and loud,
synthetic, unapologetic. A wabi-sabi colour is a *material* colour, carrying the irregularity of
the process that made it, and its surface does not stay flat.

This changes what you need around the palette. Memphis needs a strong typeface, empty space and
graphic structure — otherwise the colours fight. Wabi-sabi needs good photography, honest paper
and restraint in layout — otherwise the colours look like beige.

### 3.4 The place wabi-sabi sits

Wabi-sabi is one point in a range, with neighbours on either side.
[Nordic versus wabi-sabi](../../5-nordic/03-nordic-vs-wabi-sabi/en.md) covers the other side of
that pair: nordic shares wabi-sabi's low chroma and restraint, but is cool, clean and new. Wabi-sabi
takes that low chroma and pulls it dark and warm, toward the earth.

- **Nordic** — quiet, cool, light, new
- **Wabi-sabi** — quiet, warm, mid-dark, aged
- **Memphis** — loud, cool-and-hot at once, bright, new

Two axes do the work: chroma separates wabi-sabi from memphis, temperature separates it from
nordic. Wabi-sabi is where you land when you want low chroma *and* warmth.

## 4. Choosing between them

Choose **wabi-sabi** when:

- the product is about materials, craft, provenance
- the audience buys slowly — furniture, a print journal, a pantry
- the photography or the objects are the content, and colour should stay out of the way
- you want the brand to read honest and unhurried

Choose **memphis** when:

- the product is loud by design — a launch, a festival, a youth brand
- you need to look like you arrived recently, on purpose
- the audience expects energy and is not asked to be careful
- you want colour to be the message, not the frame

**Ask whether it gets better with age.** If yes, wabi-sabi; if it must look like it launched
last week, memphis.

## 5. Where people confuse them

**The "Muji" reflex.** Muji, and many retail interiors, are often cited as wabi-sabi, and closer
to the real thing than memphis is — but their palettes are usually *lighter* and *less chromatic*
than this one. If your swatches have no colour below `L` 0.45 and nothing in the green half of the
wheel, you are closer to a neutral retail palette than to this family.

**Calling anything desaturated "memphis".** Memphis is not a synonym for "muted but colourful";
its chroma is the highest-but-one of any family in this book. A quiet palette is wabi-sabi or
morandi, and calling it memphis is a category error.

**The retro overlap.** Both have a vintage feel and both get used for retro work, but a memphis
retro is a joke about 1981 while a wabi-sabi retro is real patina.

**Confusing wabi-sabi with morandi.** Morandi is the other low-chroma earth family, and it is
lighter: mean lightness 0.78 against wabi-sabi's 0.61, and its ink is `#3F3A38` on near-white
paper rather than `#3B342E` on lime plaster. Morandi is a still life on a table; wabi-sabi is the
room around it.

---

## 6. Using both

It is done, but only with a hard rule: the two palettes share nothing chromatically.

The workable arrangement is **separation of register**, not mixing. Put the memphis work in one
place — an announcement, a poster, a landing page, an event identity — and the wabi-sabi work in
another: product pages, the packaging, long-form editorial, the shop itself. The transition
is a deliberate contrast between loud campaign and quiet product.

What does not work is blending within one surface. A memphis squiggle on a lime-plaster card, or a
wabi-sabi photograph with memphis type on it, produces mush: the graphic element shouts at a
palette with no loud in it, and the quiet element looks timid next to one that only shouts.

One more option, used carefully: **use the black and white of memphis as the ground for the
wabi-sabi palette.** Memphis black `#1A1A1A` and white `#FFFFFF` carry no temperature of their own,
so they will not fight lime plaster or ink. That is the one place the two families genuinely
agree: a neutral ground.
