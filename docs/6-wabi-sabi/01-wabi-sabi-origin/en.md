# Wabi-sabi — where the palette comes from

---

Wabi-sabi is the quietest family in this book. Its seven colours are lime plaster, sand, clay,
bark, moss, forest and ink — every one of them a colour you could find by looking at a wall, a
riverbank or a piece of untreated wood. There is no pink in it, no blue in it, and nothing
saturated anywhere near it.

That plainness is the point, and it is also why the family is so often misapplied. A designer who
adds a filter to make things look "wabi-sabi" is using the word as a texture. The family is
actually a set of numbers: a lightness band, a chroma ceiling, four hues, and a rule about what
colour is allowed to do.

The colours themselves are in [the palette article](../02-wabi-sabi-palette/en.md); the
comparison with its loudest neighbour is in
[wabi-sabi versus memphis](../03-wabi-sabi-vs-memphis/en.md).

---

## 1. What wabi-sabi is

A wabi-sabi palette is not "brown and beige". It is a specific set of constraints:

- a **narrow lightness band** — every main colour sits between `L` 0.45 and 0.80
- a **very low chroma range** — nothing above `C` 0.05, and the floor is 0.03
- **four distinct hues** — warm earth: clay and bark, moss and forest
- **one warm ink**, and no true black anywhere in the family

The chroma range is the tightest of any family in this book: a spread of 0.02, from 0.03 to 0.05.
For comparison, macaron runs 0.06 to 0.10 and memphis runs 0.12 to 0.25. There is no version of
this palette that is more colourful, because the ceiling is the whole idea.

Note also what the band does *not* include. The lightness floor of 0.45 means nothing here is pale
to the point of disappearing — the palette has a genuinely dark end, unlike macaron or morandi.
The ceiling of 0.80 means nothing is bright either. Wabi-sabi lives in the middle of the range,
which is exactly where weathered materials live.

## 2. Where it comes from

### 2.1 Two characters

The term is two Japanese characters, and they are usually taken separately because they pull in
different directions.

**侘 (wabi)** is the harder one to translate. It refers to a deliberate rusticity — to things that
are plain, unadorned, and short of what we would normally consider sufficient. The tea ceremony is
the usual reference point: a bowl that is not perfectly symmetrical, a room with one flower in it,
an unglazed cup that is beautiful because it was fired once and no more. Wabi is austerity that
has been *chosen*, not poverty that was suffered.

**寂 (sabi)** is patina — the visible record of time on a material: the glaze that has pooled and
thinned over a hundred firings, the wood that has gone grey and soft where hands have worn it, the
iron that has bloomed. Sabi is the opposite of "new". It can only be acquired, never applied.

Together they describe a value that most design writing has no word for: the beauty of something
incomplete and unfinished, valued *because* of that rather than in spite of it.

### 2.2 The crafts the colours come from

Wabi-sabi as a named aesthetic is usually traced to the tea ceremony and the crafts that served
it, rather than to any single school or manifesto. The materials are the argument, and the
materials are what this palette quotes.

**Ceramics** did most of the work. The Japanese potters who supplied the tea world deliberately
left their surfaces unfinished: Bizen ware, fired in the kiln with the ash of the fuel falling on
it, so the surface is glazed by accident; raku, hand-formed and fired quickly in a small kiln,
crackled and smoky; shino, thick and chalky, pulled from the fire while still hot. The colour of
a Bizen piece is not applied to it. It is the colour of the ash that landed on it.

**Wood** supplied the darker end. Unfinished hinoki, weathered cedar, old beams left grey by sun
and rain — the bark and forest tones of this palette are not paint names, they are timber names.

**Textiles** supplied the mid tones and the fibre texture that carries so much of the family's
effect: undyed cotton, hemp, the irregularities of hand-woven cloth.

**Plaster** supplied the surface. Lime plaster walls, mixed with sand and sometimes straw, are
warm, slightly uneven and never one flat colour — the family takes its lightest value from here.

So a wabi-sabi palette is not an invented scheme. It is a *sampling* of materials: wall, sand,
unglazed pot, bark, moss, dark timber, ink.

## 3. The visual grammar

### 3.1 Colour comes from materials

The first rule is generative. When you need a new colour in this family, you do not pick from a
swatch library and hope. You ask what material it would be: a second firing of the same clay, the
shadowed side of a lime wall, a piece of bark still damp. The material decides the hue and the
lightness; your job is to stay inside the narrow band that produces them. This is why the family
has so few colours — a material palette is discovered, not designed, and discovery is slow.

### 3.2 Chroma stays near zero

Nothing in this family is allowed to be colourful. The band runs `C` 0.03 to 0.05 — bark, the most
chromatic of the family, is at 0.05, and it is still barely more saturated than a dark stone.

This is the single most commonly broken rule. The moment a wabi-sabi palette contains something
that reads as a colour in its own right — a terracotta orange, a sage green, a warm grey that has
become beige — it stops being wabi-sabi and becomes "muted modern". The palette's advantage is
that it cannot compete with the material or the photography for attention.

### 3.3 Warmth comes from hue, not saturation

A wabi-sabi colour is warm because of where it sits on the hue circle, not because it is
saturated. Sand at `C` 0.03 is warm; clay at `C` 0.03 is warm; even the ink, at `C` 0.01, is a
brown-black rather than a neutral black.

This is the opposite of how a warm palette is usually built, where "warm" is achieved by pushing
saturation up. Here the hue angles cluster in the 50–140 degree region — orange-browns for the
earth tones, yellow-greens for the moss and forest tones — and the chroma stays tiny. The result
is a family that reads as warm across a whole page without a single loud colour in it.

### 3.4 Texture does the work that polish would otherwise do

This is the rule that separates the family from a palette with beige in it. In a polished design
system, visual interest comes from contrast, precision and finish. In wabi-sabi, it is supposed
to come from the surface: the grain of the paper, the tooth of the plaster, the uneven light in
a photograph.

Practically: do not compensate for a quiet palette by adding decoration. Let the substrate do the
work. A photograph of a hand-thrown bowl in flat daylight, on a lime-plaster ground, with one
line of ink type under it, is a complete wabi-sabi composition. Add a gradient, a gloss, a
drop shadow and a rounded card, and you have replaced the texture with interface furniture.

## 4. The numbers

| Chroma | OKLCH C | 0.03 – 0.05 |
| Hue spread | distinct hues (30° buckets) | 4 |
| Mean lightness | mean OKLCH L | 0.61 |
| Mean chroma | mean OKLCH C | 0.04 |
| Reference: mean HSL saturation | HSL overstates pastels | 16% |
<!-- /band -->

Two numbers carry most of the meaning, and they are the opposite of macaron's. The chroma range
of `C` 0.03–0.05 is the tightest in the book — a spread of 0.02, where macaron's is 0.04 and
memphis's is 0.13. The lightness band of `L` 0.45–0.80 is narrower than swiss neutral's full grey
ladder but far wider than macaron's, because this family needs a genuinely dark end to sit
against the plaster.

Note that the band excludes the surface and the ink: it is computed from the main and accent
colours only, which is why lime plaster at `L` 0.91 and ink at `L` 0.33 sit outside it. That is
intentional and it is the family's structure — a wall, and what is written on it.

## 5. What wabi-sabi is not

- **Not "beige".** Beige is a hue at low chroma; it is one colour, and it can be any of a hundred
  slightly different ones. This family is four hues across a 0.02 chroma span, with a named
  surface and a named ink. Calling it beige is like calling macaron "pink".
- **Not a distressed filter.** A grain overlay and a desaturated photo make a modern interface
  wearing a costume. The texture is supposed to come from the material you actually chose —
  uncoated stock, unbleached cotton, a real photograph — not from noise on top of a polished design.
- **Not for products that must look new and fast.** This family says *this was made by hand, some
  time ago, and it will outlast you*. That is fine to say about a bowl and poor to say about a
  checkout flow. Wabi-sabi has no natural accent hierarchy, no "success" green, and no appetite
  for motion.
- **Not memphis.** The two are opposites in almost every measurable way, and the confusion comes
  from both being reaction styles against the same decade. See
  [wabi-sabi versus memphis](../03-wabi-sabi-vs-memphis/en.md).
- **Not a dark-mode palette.** The family assumes a light, matte ground. On a near-black surface
  these colours lose their material quality and look like a low-saturation UI theme.
