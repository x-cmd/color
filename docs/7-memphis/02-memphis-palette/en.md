# Memphis — the palette

---

The memphis palette is eight colours: one surface, four base colours, two accents and one ink. It
looks like a lot of colour for a page, and it is — but the roles are tightly assigned, and the
assignment is what keeps the page from turning into a bag of sweets.

The ramp is where memphis behaves differently from the quieter families. Because chroma here is
held at its base value while lightness moves, the tints stay vivid: a "light yellow" in this
family is still a yellow you could shout across a room. In macaron, where base chroma is around
0.06–0.09, a tint at the same lightness step would have been a whisper.

This has a direct consequence for interface work: **a tint is not a de-escalation tool here.**
You cannot make a memphis colour "quieter" by lightening it, because lightness and chroma move
independently in OKLCH and the ramp keeps its chroma. The way to calm a memphis surface is to use
less of it, or to put the black ink on it.

## 3. How the palette is put together

### 3.1 The roles

| Role | Colour | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `surface` | White `#FFFFFF` | the page, and the only large field allowed |
| `base` | Yellow `#FFE14D`, Orange `#FF9F1C`, Turquoise `#2EC4B6`, Blue `#3A86FF` | blocks, shapes, fills, chart series |
| `accent` | Hot Pink `#FF4B8B`, Violet `#8338EC` | the colours that are allowed to act |
| `ink` | Black `#1A1A1A` | all text, all lines, all rules |

That is the whole structure. There is no muted colour in this family, which is unusual — every
other family in this book has one, precisely so that captions and disabled states have somewhere
to go. Memphis solves the problem differently: secondary text is black at a smaller size, or black
on a white card, and disabled states are simply removed.

Note also that `surface` is pure white, not the near-white that swiss neutral and nordic use. The
contrast between `#FFFFFF` and the yellow is 1.30, which means a yellow block next to the page
separates by *hue*, not by *value*. That is the point.

### 3.2 Why two accents, not one

Most families in this book allow exactly one accent — the rule of thumb is one per screen, so that
the eye learns where "act" lives. Memphis breaks that rule, and the reason is the same reason it
allows six base colours: **memphis multiplies colour rather than rationing it.**

Macaron's discipline is that colour is rationed. Its base colours are decoration and its single
coral accent is the only thing on the page that means "do the thing", which is why macaron
interfaces feel calm even though they are full of pink. Memphis does not ration. It runs a high
chroma on every base colour and adds two accents on top, and the system's answer to "how does the
eye know what to click?" is not "there is only one" — it is that the accents are the *darkest and
most saturated* members of the palette and they always sit on a hard edge.

Two accents also do something structurally useful: hot pink and violet are far apart in hue
(angle 4° versus 296°), so a page can carry a pink call-to-action and a violet secondary action
without the two fighting for the same slot.

### 3.3 The imbalance

There is no dominant colour. Yellow and orange are neighbours in hue (97° and 66°) and both are
warm; blue and violet are neighbours (260° and 296°) and both are cool; pink sits at 4° with a
chroma of 0.22, one of the most saturated things in the family.

Do not assign one of them a larger share to "balance" the set. A palette where blue occupies half
the page is not a balanced memphis palette — it is a blue palette with memphis decoration. The
spread *is* the identity.

## 4. Extending it

Memphis is the easiest family in this book to extend and the easiest to ruin, because the band
is generous. Two rules keep it safe.

**Stay inside the band.** Anything with `C` between 0.12 and 0.25 and `L` between 0.55 and 0.91
belongs to the family. That covers a wide range of hues: a magenta at hue 330, a green at hue
140, a red at hue 25 will all read as memphis if their chroma and lightness are right. What will
not work is a colour at `C` 0.30 — that reads as neon, and neon is a different family with a
different ground (see [memphis versus neon](../03-memphis-vs-neon/en.md)).

**Keep the count small.** The family has six chromatic colours. Adding two more is a decision
that needs a reason, and the reason had better be a new *role* — a fifth base for a chart series
that genuinely needs one, say — rather than a fifth base because a particular shape looked better.
The failure mode of memphis is not bad colour; it is colour without hierarchy, which is what
happens when eight equal voices become twelve.

The surface and ink are not extensible at all. `#FFFFFF` and `#1A1A1A` are the ground. A "softer
memphis white" at `#F5F5F5` is fine as a panel, but if the page background becomes grey the
family loses the hard edge that makes the colour look like daylight.

## 5. Pairing rules

The contrast numbers in the palette table decide everything. Read them together and a simple
rule emerges:

| Fill | White text | Black text |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Violet `#8338EC` | 5.61 — passes | 3.74 — fails for body text |
| Blue `#3A86FF` | 3.48 — fails for body text | 6.03 — passes |
| Hot Pink `#FF4B8B` | 3.17 — fails | 6.63 — passes |
| Turquoise `#2EC4B6` | 2.17 — fails | 9.69 — passes |
| Orange `#FF9F1C` | 2.05 — fails | 10.23 — passes |
| Yellow `#FFE14D` | 1.30 — fails badly | 16.13 — passes comfortably |
| Black `#1A1A1A` | 17.40 — passes | 1.21 — fails |

So the practical rules are these:

- **Black text is safe on every colour in the family.** The darkest ratio is 3.74 against violet,
  which is below the 4.5 threshold for normal body text but fine for large text (19px bold or
  24px regular) and for non-text elements. On the other five, black passes outright.
- **White text is only ever safe on violet and on black.** Violet at 5.61 passes the 4.5 body-text
  threshold; everything else fails, most of it badly.
- **Yellow never carries white text.** 1.30 is close to invisible, and no amount of font weight
  fixes it.

This is the reverse of macaron, where the light pastels failed against white text and the ink was
the only option. Memphis has more room: black works almost everywhere, and one colour can be
flipped.

Two practical consequences. First, buttons in memphis are usually black-on-yellow or white-on-
violet, not white-on-blue — blue at 3.48 will pass as large text but will fail on a 16px label.
Second, because black works everywhere, the ink is doing less work in memphis than it does in the
pale families; the family can afford big colour blocks under black type without losing legibility,
which is exactly what a memphis poster looks like.

## 6. Common mistakes

- **Adding a third accent.** Pink and violet already split the hue circle. A third accent means
  three things are allowed to mean "act", which is how a memphis interface turns into a poster.
- **Using the loud colours for body text.** Hot pink at 3.17 against white, or turquoise at 2.17,
  will fail. Body text is black. Reserve the colours for fills and let the ink do the reading.
- **Letting the ground turn grey.** A `#F5F5F5` page with memphis colours on it loses the
  daylight quality that separates it from neon. If you want panels, put them on white, not the
  other way round.
- **Picking one colour per screen.** The instinct to give each screen a signature colour comes
  from families built around a single accent. Memphis works the opposite way: a screen with one
  memphis colour on it looks like a swatch, not like a composition.
- **Assuming a "primary" and a "secondary".** See *3.3 The imbalance* above. There is no hierarchy
  of hues in this family, and creating one breaks it.
- **Cooling it down.** Replacing the clash with a harmonious four-colour scheme produces a good
  palette that is not memphis. See [memphis versus macaron](../../2-macaron/01-macaron-origin/en.md)
  for what a rationed palette looks like.
