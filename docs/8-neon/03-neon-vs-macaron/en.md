# Neon versus Macaron

---

This is the most extreme pair in the book. Two families, both built on the idea that colour
should feel friendly rather than corporate, and almost every structural decision between them
is inverted.

Macaron is pastel on white, with one coral accent. Neon is fluorescent on near-black, with five
accents that all glow. Same lightness of *surface*, reversed. Same "sweet" reading of colour,
turned all the way up and then set on black.

What makes the pair interesting is that they are not opposites in the way "good versus bad"
pairs are. Both are one-system families, and both discipline their hue counts hard. Once you see
that, the comparison stops being a taste question and starts being a question about what the
colour is *for*.

The two families are described in [macaron — where the palette comes
from](../../2-macaron/01-macaron-origin/en.md) and [macaron — the
palette](../../2-macaron/02-macaron-palette/en.md), and in
[neon — where the palette comes from](../01-neon-origin/en.md) and
[neon — the palette](../02-neon-palette/en.md). The other side of this pair — the comparison
with the other loud family — is in [memphis versus neon](../../7-memphis/03-memphis-vs-neon/en.md).

## 1. The short version

Macaron says: *this colour is a treat*. Neon says: *this colour is powered*.

Macaron's argument is that colour should be edible — the palette is named after a French pastry,
and the colours come from the pastry's shells, which are pale while the flavour is not. Neon's
argument is that colour should be luminous — the palette is named after a gas tube, and the
colours come from light passing through excited gas.

That difference propagates into everything:

| | Macaron | Neon |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ground | cream paper `#FFFDFB`, near-white | void `#05060A`, near-black |
| Surface value | `L` 1.00 | `L` 0.12 |
| Chroma | 0.06 – 0.10 | 0.02 – 0.29 |
| Mean chroma | 0.07 | 0.18 |
| Accents | one — coral `#FFAAA5` | five — cyan, magenta, lime, violet, amber |
| Ink | plum `#4A4045`, `L` 0.38 | near-white `#E6EDF3`, `L` 0.94 |
| What colour means | decoration, material | light source, signal |

The single sharpest number in the table is the chroma. Macaron's ceiling is 0.10; neon runs to
0.29 — nearly three times as saturated. These are not the same colours at different levels.
They are different substances.

## 2. Side by side

The most useful way to see this is to put the same *role* in each family and ask what colour
does it.

**The surface.** Macaron's is `#FFFDFB` at `L` 1.00 — a warm cream, not white, because the
family never uses a truly neutral. Neon's is `#05060A` at `L` 0.12 — a near-black with a blue
cast, not neutral either. Both families tint their ground, in the same way: a ground with a
temperature reads as a material, a ground without one reads as a void. Macaron's is the paper
you're on; neon's is the street outside.

**The accent.** Macaron has exactly one: coral `#FFAAA5`. It is the button, the active tab, the
selected chip. Because everything else is so quiet, coral does not need to be saturated to be
seen — it needs to be *alone*. Neon has five, and the same logic runs in reverse: because the
ground is so dark, the accents need to be saturated and separated to be counted as separate
things. Neon magenta `#FF2E88` and neon violet `#9B5CFF` are the ones that most need a reason
to exist, because they sit closest to the family it might otherwise be mistaken for.

**The ink.** Macaron's ink is a plum `#4A4045` — a dark, warm-neutral colour, `L` 0.38. It is
part of the palette, not a neutral imported from outside it. Neon's ink is `#E6EDF3` at `L`
0.94 — a cool near-white. Same principle, opposite direction: macaron's text is a dark colour
that belongs to the family; neon's text is a light colour that belongs to the family.

**The muted role.** Macaron's dusty rose `#8C7F84` at `L` 0.61 and neon's muted `#8B98A5` at
`L` 0.67 are closer than any other pairing in this comparison — same lightness, both
desaturated, both tinted toward their own family's temperature. This is the closest the two
families come, and it is worth noticing: the *secondary* text role is a universal idea, and only
the temperature changes.

## 3. Where they really differ

### 3.1 The ground is the argument

Every other difference follows from the ground, and this is why "just invert the macaron
palette" does not produce neon.

If you take macaron's colours and put them on black, you do not get neon; you get macaron in
the dark, which reads as a mistake. The pastel colours glow on black, but they glow *flat* —
they have no hierarchy against a surface that is nearly as light as they are in some places, and
the whole point of neon is a hierarchy of light sources.

The reverse also fails. Neon's accents on white are genuinely hard to read: neon magenta
`#FF2E88` reaches a contrast ratio of 6.00 against black but only 3.50 against white. Neon's
text `#E6EDF3` reaches 17.77 against the void and 1.18 against white — on a light page it is
invisible. The family was built for a black ground and cannot be relocated.

### 3.2 Chroma is the real dividing line

Macaron's chroma ceiling of 0.10 is not really about restraint for its own sake. It is about
what pastel means: a pale surface that promises a strong taste. The strong taste is in the
filling, not the shell. Macaron keeps its hues legible — you can name each one, pink or mint or
lemon — by never pushing them past the point where the name stops applying.

Neon's chroma range to 0.29 says something else: the colour *is* the event. Acid lime at `C`
0.29 and magenta at `C` 0.24 are not describing a flavour; they are emitting.

### 3.3 What the accents mean

Macaron's coral accent means "act". One colour, one meaning, one job — it is the button.

Neon's five accents each mean something slightly different, and this is a real difference in
kind, not just in quantity. Acid lime is the one that reads most unmistakably as light; cyan is
the technical one, the colour of a terminal or a link; amber is the sole warm note; magenta and
violet are the hot ones. In macaron, colour is decorative and the accent is functional. In neon,
the accents are the content and they are functional too, but they carry several different
meanings at once.

### 3.4 Both are one-system families

This is the part that is easy to miss and the part that makes the comparison worth reading.

Macaron is not "pastels". It is seven named hues at one lightness, one accent, and a rule that
ink is never a fill. Neon is not "bright colours on dark". It is two near-blacks, five fixed
accents, and a rule that the ground never moves. Both families exist to prevent their palette
from accumulating, and both solve it the same way: by naming the hues in advance and holding
the count.

So the comparison is not "structured versus unstructured". It is two disciplined systems that
disagree about almost every other thing.

## 4. Choosing between them

Choose **macaron** when the product is about consumption and the audience is comfortable with
warmth. Consumer apps, food and drink, children's products, seasonal campaigns, onboarding and
empty states — the cases where a soft ground and a single friendly accent do more work than
they could hurt. It also works where you will print, where the content is read in daylight, and
where a form has to be filled in.

Choose **neon** when the product is about presence. Nightlife, music and gaming, developer tools
that want an edge, portfolio and landing pages, terminal-adjacent products. It works when the
audience arrives at night, when the surface wants to feel like a space rather than a document,
and when short bursts of high-contrast colour will be read comfortably but long sessions will
not.

There is one more question worth asking, and it is the one that decides more cases than taste
does: **what happens on a light background?** If the product will ever be read in daylight, on a
printed page, or in a context where the user can switch to a light theme, macaron's ground is
already there and neon's is not. Neon's family is not dark mode — it does not have a light
variant waiting. That is a real constraint, not a preference.

## 5. Where people confuse them

**"Neon is just macaron for men."** This is the most common confusion and it is entirely
backwards. Both families are colour-as-decoration; macaron is simply better at it. What neon
adds is a ground, a hierarchy of light sources, and a set of functional accent colours — none
of which macaron has any use for.

**"A neon accent on white is fine because the colour is bright."** Bright is not the same as
legible. Neon magenta on white is 3.50, which is below the 4.5 threshold for body text and
barely above the 3 for large text. Neon cyan on white would be 1.41, and neon's own text colour
on white is 1.18 — effectively invisible.

**"Macaron and neon share a hue, so they are related."** Coral `#FFAAA5` sits at hue 23° and
neon magenta `#FF2E88` at 2° — close enough to look like the same colour in a swatch grid. But
one is at `L` 0.82 with `C` 0.10 and the other at `L` 0.66 with `C` 0.24. What differs is
exactly the part that matters: chroma, and what the family does with it.

**"Memphis is the family that looks like neon."** Memphis is also loud, also 1980s, also uses
saturated colour against a dark ground. But Memphis is loud in daylight and on purpose, with a
white in the palette, squiggles and geometry doing the work. Neon is quiet, nocturnal and
photographic. The full comparison is in
[memphis versus neon](../../7-memphis/03-memphis-vs-neon/en.md).

## 6. Using both

They can share a page, but only if you keep them in separate rooms.

The workable arrangement is a macaron product with a neon moment in it — a launch page, an
event microsite, a campaign microsite embedded in a consumer app. In that case macaron handles
the interface and neon handles the hero, and the transition between them should be a hard cut
rather than a blend. Do not use macaron pastels inside a neon section; the two grounds are
different enough that a pastel on black reads as an error rather than as a link between
sections.

The reverse — neon as the system with macaron as an accent — does not work. Macaron's colours
are built around a light ground; dropping them onto a neon panel gives you colours with no
light source, which is the one thing the neon half cannot accommodate.

What does work is the accent trick in the other direction: neon's own accent discipline is
stricter than macaron's, so if you are building a neon product and want one friendly moment
in it, take macaron's *coral* as a single accent and drop it on the neon ground. It will read
as a warm object lit by the tubes rather than as a pastel, and it is the one macaron colour
that survives the move.
