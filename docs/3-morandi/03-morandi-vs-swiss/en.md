# Morandi versus Swiss Neutral

---

These are the two quiet families in this book, and they are quieter than they look. Morandi is warm,
muted and material. Swiss neutral is achromatic, exact and structural. Both refuse to shout. They
get there from opposite directions, and the differences matter the moment you put them on the same
page.

This article compares them. The morandi palette itself is in
[the morandi palette](../02-morandi-palette/en.md); the swiss neutral one is in
[the swiss neutral palette](../../4-swiss-neutral/02-swiss-neutral-palette/en.md), and its history
is in [swiss neutral origin](../../4-swiss-neutral/01-swiss-neutral-origin/en.md). The comparison on
morandi's other side, with macaron, is in
[macaron versus morandi](../../2-macaron/03-macaron-vs-morandi/en.md).

---

## 1. The short version

Both families are restrained. What they restrain is different.

**Morandi** is grey pulled toward colour. Every one of its ten members has a hue and a warmth: the
paper is `#F5F2ED` at hue 81°, the ink is `#3F3A38` at hue 43°, and the mid-tones are sage, clay,
mist blue and dusty mauve. Its accent, umber `#8C7A6E`, is deep and earthy and does structural
work. Its lightness range is wide: `L` 0.59 to 0.90, so it can hold both a pale ground and a dark
block.

**Swiss neutral** is grey as grey. Six of its seven members are literally achromatic — paper
`#FFFFFF`, panel `#F5F5F5`, rule `#E0E0E0`, secondary `#9B9B9B`, deep `#333333` and black
`#000000` — all at `C` 0.00. Its one chromatic member is a signal red `#FF0000` at `C` 0.26,
which is the loudest thing in either family by a factor of four. Its lightness range is much wider
still, `L` 0.32 to 0.97, and that range is a fixed ladder of greys, not an open field.

So: morandi is a palette of warm, dulled earth colours that happens to include a deep structural
tone. Swiss neutral is a monochrome system with exactly one permitted exception.

## 2. Side by side

| | Morandi | Swiss neutral |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ground | `#F5F2ED`, warm | `#FFFFFF`, pure |
| Lightness band | `L` 0.59 – 0.90 | `L` 0.32 – 0.97 |
| Chroma band | `C` 0.01 – 0.06 | `C` 0.00 – 0.26 |
| Distinct hues | 4 | 1 |
| Mean lightness | 0.78 | 0.71 |
| Mean chroma | 0.03 | 0.06 |
| The accent | umber `#8C7A6E`, structural | signal red `#FF0000`, declarative |
| Body text | ink `#3F3A38`, warm | black `#000000` or deep `#333333` |
| Best at | editorial pages, galleries, lifestyle brands | documentation, architecture studios, reporting |

Read the hue row first. Swiss neutral's single hue is not a limitation on how much grey you have —
it is a statement that the colour in this family is *rationed*. Its mean chroma of 0.06 is higher
than morandi's 0.03, but that number comes entirely from one member. Strip out the signal red and
swiss neutral's mean chroma falls to zero.

## 3. Where they really differ

### 3.1 Warm-tinted grey against true grey

This is the difference you can feel before you can name it. Morandi's neutrals are not grey; they
are warm colours that happen to be nearly achromatic. Its paper carries a hue of 81° — yellow-red
— at `C` 0.01. Swiss neutral's paper is `#FFFFFF`, its panel `#F5F5F5`, its rule `#E0E0E0`: all at
hue 90° with `C` 0.00 exactly.

The consequence is a different kind of adjacency. A cool colour next to a warm grey sits still.
A cool colour next to a true grey grows an edge — the eye reads a colour difference where there is
only a lightness difference. Put mist blue `#B0B7C6` on a Swiss panel and it will look bluer than
it is; put it on a morandi paper `#F5F2ED` and it settles.

This is why morandi can put two or three of its own hues in the same composition and Swiss neutral
rarely tries: with a truly neutral ground, every chromatic element has to fight for the eye.

### 3.2 A soft accent against a hard one

Both families have exactly one accent, and they use the word in completely different senses.

Morandi's umber is `C` 0.03. It is the darkest base in the palette at `L` 0.59, and it is used for
rules, for the darker end of a fill, for small-caps labels, for the secondary action. Its contrast
against the paper surface is 3.67, and 4.10 against white — enough for large text and non-text
elements, not enough for body copy. It is a structural colour that happens to be the accent in the
role table.

Swiss neutral's signal red is `C` 0.26, more than four times morandi's most saturated base. It is
`#FF0000` — the crudest, most unapologetic red available. It sits at 4.00 against white and 5.25
against black. The rule that governs it is not "use it sparingly" but "there is only one, and when
it appears it means *act*". It never decorates.

The practical test: in Swiss neutral, if red is on the page, something is happening. In morandi, if
umber is on the page, something is being organised. Same slot, opposite jobs.

### 3.3 Range against a ladder

Morandi's lightness band spans `L` 0.59 to 0.90 — a wide range for a family whose chroma ceiling
is 0.06. Swiss neutral's spans `L` 0.32 to 0.97, wider still, but it is a **fixed ladder**: paper,
panel, rule, secondary, deep, black. You pick steps from it. Morandi's range is a field of
possibilities bounded by a chroma ceiling instead.

That is the more useful way to see the difference. Macaron and morandi both work by holding chroma
down and moving lightness; Swiss neutral works by holding chroma at exactly zero and moving
lightness across a prescribed ladder. The first two leave you somewhere to experiment. Swiss neutral
deliberately leaves you nowhere.

### 3.4 The grid against the material

Swiss neutral's actual subject is not colour at all. It is typography and layout: the Swiss
International Typographic Style, with its grid, its asymmetry and its Helvetica. The palette is
almost incidental — a greyscale ladder plus a red, chosen so the layout has something to point at.
That is why its band is wide: the greys have to support a range of typographic weights.

Morandi's subject is closer to the surface. It reads as material — paper, plaster, clay, stone —
and its wide lightness band exists so a page can have both a pale ground and a dark structural
block, the way a painted wall has both a lit face and a shadow.

## 4. Choosing between them

Ask one question: **should the reader feel the page, or should they read it?**

If the page is the point — a gallery, a portfolio, a lifestyle brand, an editorial layout where the
photography and the paper should feel like a material — choose morandi. Its warmth comes free with
the palette, and it will not fight the images.

If the content is the point — documentation, a technical spec, an architecture studio's project
archive, a data report — choose Swiss neutral. Its discipline is the point: a hard grid, one red,
and a greyscale that gets out of the way.

There are two sharper tests.

**Does the page need an obvious action?** Swiss neutral has an answer — its red. Morandi does not,
and pretending otherwise is how morandi layouts end up with a coral button that violates the
chroma ceiling.

**Does the content need a lot of colours to be told apart?** If you have more than four or five
things that must be distinguishable at a glance, neither family is the right answer, and morandi is
the wronger one, because its whole mechanism is that things do not distinguish themselves.

## 5. Where people confuse them

**"Morandi is just Swiss neutral with a warmer cast."** It is warmer, but it also has four hues and
a chroma ceiling. Adding three greened or blued greys to a Swiss palette gives you something that
looks Swiss but is not governed by Swiss rules — it is a morandi palette with an incomplete hue
set, which is a bad morandi palette.

**"They are both beige systems."** Swiss neutral's greys are cool and pure. `#F5F5F5` and `#F5F2ED`
are close in lightness — 0.97 against 0.96 — but the Swiss one is a true neutral and the morandi
one is warm. On screen the difference is obvious; in a printed grey scale it partly disappears.

**"Morandi's umber is the same idea as Swiss neutral's signal red."** Same slot, opposite intent.
Red is a signal to act; umber is a structure to organise by. If you are using umber to attract
attention, you are using it wrong.

**"A morandi page and a Swiss page can share a palette."** They can share a *ground* — a warm paper
works fine under a Swiss grid — but the moment you add Swiss red to a morandi page you have two
accents, one of them outside the family.

---

## 6. Using both

This is more common than it should be, and it works when the two are kept in separate rooms.

The natural division is **ground versus accent**. Take a Swiss layout — grid, Helvetica-scale
type, hard alignment — and warm its ground: use morandi's paper `#F5F2ED` for the page, oat
`#E3DDD3` for panels, and ink `#3F3A38` instead of pure black for body text. Keep Swiss
discipline everywhere else, and keep a single accent.

The other division is **image versus interface**. A gallery site can run morandi throughout the
editorial and gallery surfaces, and use a Swiss-neutral treatment for a booking form or a filter
panel at the end of a page — the part where the reader is transacting rather than looking. The
interface half has rules a Swiss system can enforce: one action colour, labelled fields, a visible
focus ring.

What breaks it is sharing the *accents*. If morandi contributes umber and Swiss contributes red,
the page has two structural colours competing for the same job, and the result is neither family.
Pick one accent for the whole page.
