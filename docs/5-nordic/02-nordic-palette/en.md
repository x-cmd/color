# Nordic — the palette

---

Nine colours. One is the surface, four are the cool bases, three are warm accents, one is the ink.
That is the whole system, and the smallness of the number is the point: a family this restrained is
one you can hold in your head across a whole product.

The colours are introduced in [nordic — where the palette comes from](../01-nordic-origin/en.md),
which covers why they are the way they are. This article is the reference — what each colour is
for, how to extend it, and how to pair it without letting the page go cold.

The ramp is generated by moving lightness in even steps along the OKLCH lightness axis while holding
hue and chroma fixed. That is a deliberate choice rather than the usual HSL tint-and-shade, because
moving `L` in OKLCH produces steps that *look* evenly spaced — the lightness difference between two
adjacent rows is the same wherever you are on the ramp.

Two consequences matter in practice. First, a deep step of a pale blue stays recognisably the same
blue rather than sliding towards grey, which is what happens when you darken in HSL. Second, the
tint 40 column of a warm accent stays warm; you get a genuinely lighter terracotta, not a pinkish
thing that has drifted off the hue.

## 3. How the palette is put together

### 3.1 The four blues are one hue

Four of the nine colours — frost `#E8EDF0`, glacier `#C9D6DE`, fjord `#9FB6C4` and deep fjord
`#6B8FA3` — sit between hue 233° and 235° in OKLCH. Their chroma runs from 0.01 to 0.05. They are
not four colours. They are four lightness steps of one blue-grey, and the eye reads them as one
family for exactly that reason.

Their division of labour is by function, not by decoration:

| Colour | Role | Where it goes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| frost `#E8EDF0` | panel | card and sidebar fills, table header rows, hover states |
| glacier `#C9D6DE` | rule | borders, dividers, chart gridlines, disabled outlines |
| fjord `#9FB6C4` | secondary | secondary text, muted labels, unselected tabs, chart secondary series |
| deep fjord `#6B8FA3` | support | links, active tab underline, chart primary series, the darkest blue that still fits the band |

Because the four sit on a single hue, they can be layered freely. A frost card on a snow page
separated by a glacier border is a complete, legible interface with no third colour involved.

### 3.2 The three warm accents

Terracotta `#D9A38F`, eucalyptus `#A9BC8F` and mustard `#E3C46A` are the only members of the family
with meaningful chroma: `C` 0.07, `C` 0.07 and `C` 0.12. Mustard, at `C` 0.12, is the single most
saturated colour in the set — and it is still less than half as chromatic as Memphis blue.

They are the only saturated colours because they are doing the one job the blues cannot do. A page
of nothing but blue-greys reads as cold and slightly clinical; the warm accents are the note that
keeps it human. Warm colour also reads as closer and softer, which is why a terracotta button feels
gentler than a blue one at the same size.

The three are not interchangeable:

- **terracotta** is the warmest — a red-shifted clay. Use it for states that want to feel human
  and firm.
- **mustard** is the brightest and the most saturated. It is the family's one "look here" colour
  and it should be rare.
- **eucalyptus** sits between the blues and the warms, and is the most versatile. Its `C` 0.07 and
  hue of 126° make it read as a soft sage green rather than a colour. Use it where you want a
  second accent that does not compete.

### 3.3 What the ink is doing

Ink `#2E3A40` is not black. It is a blue-black: hue 230°, `L` 0.34, and it is the only colour in
the family darker than `L` 0.63. Text in Nordic is never neutral grey, for the same reason the
neutrals are never neutral grey — the whole page is one temperature.

It measures 11.70 against white, so it passes comfortably as body text on any of the pale
surfaces — snow, frost, glacier and fjord are all lighter than white is, and ink against fjord
still leaves a wide margin.

Note what Nordic does *not* have: there is no separate muted colour, no `#8A817A`-style secondary
ink. If you need a secondary text colour, it is fjord `#9FB6C4` — and you should check the
contrast on the particular surface before shipping it, because fjord at 2.11 against white is a
light colour doing a text job.

## 4. Extending it

The Nordic hue set has four members and it is small **on purpose**. The extension rule follows
directly: **a new colour is a new tint of an existing hue, not a new hue.**

If you need a colour that is not in the palette, walk down the ramp of the closest hue instead of
introducing a new one. A darker terracotta for a pressed state, a paler eucalyptus for a background
band, a lighter blue for a hover fill — all of these are ramp steps, and all of them stay inside the
band.

When you do need to go off-palette, hold the three constraints in this order:

1. **Stay in the band.** `L` 0.63 to 0.94 for anything that is not ink. Anything darker and the
   family starts reading as swiss neutral or morandi.
2. **Stay under the chroma ceiling.** `C` 0.12. If you find yourself at `C` 0.16 and thinking it
   still looks Nordic, it does not.
3. **Reuse an existing hue.** A new hue at a low chroma reads as an accidental neutral; at a high
   chroma, as a different family entirely.

There are two honest exceptions. The first is a semantic colour — an error red, a success green.
Nordic has no red, and no amount of tinting will produce one, so an error state needs a colour from
outside the family or a change of form (an icon, a border weight). The second is a dark-mode
surface, which is a different problem with different rules; the band in this article assumes a
light ground.

## 5. Pairing rules

### 5.1 Which colour may carry text

There is a clean answer, and it comes straight from the contrast numbers in the table above. The
columns are measured against pure white and pure black; a Nordic surface is a touch off white, so
the number against white is the conservative case.

| Colour | Against white | Against black | May carry body text? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ink `#2E3A40` | 11.70 | 1.80 | Yes — the body text colour, everywhere |
| deep fjord `#6B8FA3` | 3.45 | 6.08 | Yes on light surfaces — the one blue that works as text |
| fjord `#9FB6C4` | 2.11 | 9.96 | No as body text on a light surface |
| terracotta `#D9A38F` | 2.19 | 9.59 | Only with dark text on top of it |
| eucalyptus `#A9BC8F` | 2.04 | 10.28 | Only with dark text on top of it |
| mustard `#E3C46A` | 1.70 | 12.37 | Only with dark text on top of it |

Ink is the body text and it stays ink. Deep fjord `#6B8FA3` at 6.08 against black is the one base
colour that works as text on a light ground, and it is the usual choice for links and for the active
item in a navigation list — where the text needs to look like text but not like body copy. Its 3.45
against white also clears the 3:1 threshold for non-text elements such as focus rings and chart
marks.

The other four are fills. When you put text on terracotta, eucalyptus or mustard, the text is ink,
not white: white text on mustard is a contrast ratio of 1.70 and is unreadable at any size. Each
of the three accents clears 9.59 against black, so ink on any of them is comfortable.

### 5.2 The 3-to-1 rule for surfaces

Where a coloured fill sits next to a text colour, the standard is 3:1 for non-text elements such as
borders, chart bars and focus rings. Two of the family's own pairs sit at or near that line:

- **glacier `#C9D6DE` as a rule on snow `#F7F9FA`** is a border, not a background, and its role is
  to be seen rather than read. It works as long as nothing else on the screen needs to carry text.
- **deep fjord `#6B8FA3` against snow**, at 3.45, clears the threshold for a link, an underline or
  a chart series, which is why deep fjord rather than fjord is the family's link colour.

The rule that follows: **structure comes from the blue ramp, emphasis comes from a warm accent, and
only two colours ever sit on top of each other.** A page where a mustard chip holds terracotta text
is a page that has misread the family.

### 5.3 Rules of thumb

- **One accent per screen.** Not one accent *type* — one instance. A single mustard button among
  frost and glacier is the family's signature; two mustard buttons on one screen turns the page
  cheerful.
- **Accents on the accent colour.** If terracotta is your accent, the link colour is terracotta
  and the active state is terracotta, not deep fjord as well.
- **The blue ramp does the structure.** If a page needs four different blues at once, it needs
  fewer elements.
- **Never a pure grey.** Every neutral in the family is a tint of the blue or the green. If you
  need a neutral, use glacier or fjord.

## 6. Common mistakes

**Too many blues.** This is the most common failure, and it is easy to miss because the result
still looks calm. Adding a second blue — a teal, a steel, a navy that breaches the band — gives
you five blues where the family intended four steps of one. The page stops reading as one system
and starts reading as a collection. The fix is to check whether the new blue can be expressed as a
ramp step of an existing one.

**Warm accents overused.** Terracotta and mustard are the family's only saturated colours, so every
extra instance is a real cost. The failure mode is visible: mustard on a badge, mustard on a
button, mustard on a link, mustard on a chart highlight. The page was probably meant to feel calm
and now feels cheerful. Use one accent colour on a screen, and pick one.

**Pale colours washing out in dense layouts.** This is where Nordic genuinely fails, and it fails
predictably. A table with a frost header and glacier row borders and fjord secondary text has
three pale values doing three different jobs within a few hundred pixels of each other. On a good
monitor that works. On a projector, on a cheap laptop panel, or printed in greyscale, the row
structure disappears. The remedy is to add structure with something other than lightness — a
weight, a rule, a spacing rhythm — and to reserve the palest steps for genuinely large areas
rather than for hairline separations.

**Cooling the warm accents out.** Terracotta at `C` 0.07 and mustard at `C` 0.12 are already
restrained. Desaturating them further to make the palette "more consistent" leaves you with a
family that is entirely cool, which is the one thing the accents exist to prevent.

**Using ink as a fill.** Ink is at `L` 0.34, well below the band. A large block of it will read as
swiss neutral — a hard dark panel with pale text — which is a different design language entirely.
